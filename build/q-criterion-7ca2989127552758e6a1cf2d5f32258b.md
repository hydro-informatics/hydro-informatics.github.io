## What the **Q-criterion** is  

For any 3-D velocity field **u = (u, v, w)** you can split the velocity-gradient tensor  
$$
\nabla\mathbf{u}= \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}
$$
into its **symmetric** part **S** (the strain–rate tensor) and **antisymmetric** part **Ω** (the rotation/vorticity tensor):

$$
S_{ij}=\tfrac12\!\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}+\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right),\qquad
\Omega_{ij}=\tfrac12\!\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}-\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)
$$

The *second invariant* of ∇u,  
$$
Q=\tfrac12\!\left(\|\Omega\|^{2}-\|S\|^{2}\right)
     =\tfrac14\bigl(\omega_i\omega_i-2S_{ij}S_{ij}\bigr)
$$
(where **ω** = ∇ × u) measures the excess of local rotation over strain.  
* **Q > 0** → rotation dominates → a vortex core  
* **Q < 0** → strain dominates → shear layers, saddle regions

Because the definition is Galilean-invariant (independent of the observer’s translational motion) it is a robust way to tag coherent vortical structures in unsteady flows.

---

## Why it matters for surface-water turbulence

| Aspect | Practical meaning in open-channel / free-surface CFD |
|--------|-----------------------------------------------------|
| **Energy cascade** | Large eddies generated at the bed or lateral shear layers show up as *Q > 0* tubes or "mushrooms"; watching their breakup helps check whether the grid is fine enough to capture energy down-cascade. |
| **Free-surface imprint** | Vertical vortices emerging from bed roughness often penetrate the free surface; plotting *Q* iso-surfaces that intersect *z = η(x,y,t)* reveals air-entrainment pockets or roller footprints in hydraulic jumps. |
| **Mixing & dispersion** | Regions of persistent positive *Q* coincide with enhanced vertical exchange; useful for pollutant or sediment modules that rely on turbulent diffusivity estimates. |
| **Model evaluation** | In LES/DNS you expect a broad spectrum of small-scale *Q* structures; if you run RANS, only the time-averaged, larger-scale vortices survive. Comparing the two tells you what the turbulence closure is suppressing. |

Tubes: Think of a Q-tube as the skin of a vortex core. Watching how that skin stretches, thins and finally tears tells you whether your numerical mesh is letting the turbulence cascade work naturally—or whether it is being strangled early by numerical diffusion. If the breakup occurs smoothly and at a scale that is still several cells across, you are in good shape; if not, it is a cue to revisit grid density, aspect ratio, or the discretisation scheme (read more below -- at the bottom of this chapter).

# Why big tubes should break up

| Flow regime | What *really* happens | What you should see in Q-iso surfaces |
|-------------|-----------------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Fully-turbulent open-channel or estuarine LES/DNS** | Horseshoe vortices form at the bed, then stretch and undergo the Richardson cascade | A continuous tube that pinches, kinks and spawns a family of smaller tubes downstream |
| **Coarse LES / VLES / WMLES** | Only the first one or two cascade steps are resolved; the SGS model removes the rest | Tubes survive but develop a fuzzy outer halo—no pepper"” of tiny blobs |
| **RANS / depth-averaged models** | Everything below the mixing-length is modeled | You will *not* see breakup; that is expected |

So for scale-resolving simulations the breakup is *desirable*—it means your mesh is letting at least the energy-containing and part of the inertial subrange develop.

## 2  Quick visual checklist

1. **Threshold choice** – plot 5 – 25 % of the positive Q-range (or ≈ 1 × Q<sub>rms</sub>); that keeps focus on energetic cores  ([No Slide Title](https://www.tfd.chalmers.se/~lada/comp_turb_model/postscript_files/Quick_Guide_to_Setting_Up_LES_version_1.4_for_Lars.pdf)).  
2. **Coherence length** – until the local eddy diameter ≈ 6–10 Δ (cell size) the tube should be smooth; if it dissolves at, say, 20 Δ, numerical diffusion is excessive .  
3. **Grid imprint** – checkerboard"” Q-surfaces or eddies aligned with I-J-K axes are red flags for anisotropic cells.  
4. **Refinement test** – double the resolution in the shear-layer zone; if the breakup point moves upstream or the diameter distribution extends to smaller scales, the original mesh was under-resolved.

## 3  Quantitative mesh-quality indicators based on Q

| Metric (all can be scripted) | Good rule-of-thumb | Why it works |
|-----------------------------|--------------------|--------------|
| **Eddy count N(d)** – number of disconnected Q-clusters vs. equivalent diameter *d* | N(d) collapses (±10 %) between two successive mesh refinements over the range 2Δ ≤ d ≤ L<sub>0</sub>/2 | Detects whether you pick up the first and second cascade generations |
| **Resolved-energy ratio** (Pope / Celik *LESIQ* index) | IQ<sub>k</sub> or LESIQ > 0.8 in zones of interest | > 80 % of TKE carried by resolved scales means most *visible* Q-structures are physical  |
| **Iso-volume V(Q<sub>iso</sub>)** | Change < ±5 % when Δt shrinks or mesh refines | Filters out aliasing by tying result to one fixed threshold |
| **PDF of |Q|** | High-Q tail slope converges; kurtosis stops growing with Δ-refinement | Spots spurious overshoots caused by dispersive numerics |

*Implementation hint* – both ParaView and PyVista can export connected-component labels; feed that list to `numpy.bincount` to build N(d) in a few lines.

---

## 4  How fine is fine" enough”?—practical numbers

* **Integral-scale criterion** – aim for at least 10–15 cells across the local integral length‐scale *l₀* (≈ 0.4 h in straight rivers; smaller behind sluices) to resolve ≈ 80 % of TKE .  
* **Wall resolution** – WRLES: y⁺ ≤ 1; WMLES: y⁺ ≤ 40, but keep Δ<sub>x,z</sub>/Δ<sub>y</sub> < 10 to avoid pancake"” cells.  
* **Free surface** – vertical Δ ≈ h/50 keeps Kelvin–Helmholtz rollers visible right up to the air–water interface.  
* **Time step** – CFL ≲ 0.5; otherwise tubes break numerically rather than physically.

---

## 5  Fixing unwanted behaviour

| Symptom | Likely cause | Remedies |
|---------|-------------|----------|
| Tubes disappear *too soon* (no small-scale offspring) | Excess numerical dissipation (1st-order schemes, over-stretched cells) | Switch to central/linear schemes, locally refine, keep aspect ratio < 3 |
| Tubes shatter into grid-aligned glitter"” right after birth | Δ too coarse relative to *l₀*; aliasing | Isotropic refinement or adaptive re-meshing driven by *|Q| > Q<sub>thr</sub>* |
| Breakup point shifts with time-step size | Temporal under-resolution | Cut Δt or use second-order implicit |
| LESIQ < 0.6 in bed layer | SGS model doing all the work | Refine until LESIQ ≥ 0.8 or switch to wall-modelled LES |

Advanced option: **adaptive refinement on Q** – many solvers (OpenFOAM `dynamicRefineFvMesh`, Basilisk `adapt_wavelet`) let you tag cells where |Q| > α Q′ and keep Δ below a user set maximum; that targets vortices without blowing up cell count elsewhere.

## 6  Putting it together: a minimal workflow

1. **Baseline run** with your current grid.  
2. Post-process **Q**, build the `Q_Criterion_Surface_Water.ipynb` notebook’s histogram or N(d) spectrum.  
3. **Local refinement** (×2) in zones where Q>α Q<sub>rms</sub>.  
4. Repeat run; compare spectra and IQ<sub>k</sub>.  
5. If spectra match and IQ<sub>k</sub> ≳ 0.8, grid is adequate; else iterate.

Following these steps will turn the qualitative mushroom" watching” into a repeatable grid-independence test, and your surface-water LES will stand on solid ground.

2. **Filtering / smoothing**  
   A narrow stencil acts like a high-pass filter and may light up numerical wiggles as false vortices. A simple remedy is to *Gaussian-smooth* Q once before contouring.

3. **Post-processing tools**  
   *ParaView* and *Tecplot* calculate **Q** internally: **Filters → Gradient Of Unstructured Data → Q-Criterion**.  
   For Python, a few lines with *numpy* or *xarray* suffice:
   ```python
   # u, v, w on 3-D array; dx, dy, dz arrays
   du_dx = np.gradient(u, dx, axis=0)
   du_dy = np.gradient(u, dy, axis=1)
   ...
   S = 0.5*((du_dx + du_dx.T) + ...)
   Omega = 0.5*((du_dx - du_dx.T) + ...)
   Q = 0.5*(np.sum(Omega**2,axis=(0,1)) - np.sum(S**2,axis=(0,1)))
   ```

4. **Choosing an iso-level**  
   The conventional choice is *Q = 0*; in practice you pick *Q = α Q′* where *Q′* is the domain rms and *α ≈ 1–2*. That suppresses tiny, non-energetic eddies.

### 4. Caveats specific to **shallow-water / quasi-2-D** models

* In depth-averaged (2-DH) simulations, Ω collapses to a scalar vorticity **ωz**. The full tensor invariants no longer exist, so the Q-criterion is **not** defined—use **ωz** or Okubo–Weiss instead.
* In Stratified rivers or estuaries, vertical shear dominates over spanwise rotation; Q may under-diagnose baroclinic"” vortices tied to density gradients.
* Near hydraulic structures (piers, gates) flow separation becomes strongly 3-D—here the Q-criterion regains value even if the far-field is essentially shallow-water.

---

### 5. Relationship to other vortex identifiers

| Criterion | Definition | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|-----------|------------|
| **λ₂** (Jeong-Hussain) | 2nd eigenvalue of **S²+Ω²** < 0 | Ignores unsteady strain | Needs eigensolve |
| **Δ-criterion** | 3rd invariant of ∇u | Fast to compute | Sensitive to threshold |
| **Swirling strength (λci)** | Imaginary part of complex-conjugate eigenpairs of ∇u | Direct swirl"” magnitude | Not Galilean-invariant under shear |

For surface-water LES, Q and λ₂ usually correlate; Q is simpler to implement and interpret.

3. **Couple with scalar fields**: overlay dye concentration or TKE; intense mixing occurs where scalar gradients coincide with *Q > 0* tubes.

**What the tubes"” are**

When you plot an isosurface of **Q > 0** (pick a fixed threshold such as ≈ 1 × Q<sub>rms</sub>) you get a three-dimensional *boundary* that encloses every point where local rotation beats strain.  
* If the underlying vortex is reasonably compact, this boundary has an almost circular cross-section and stretches along the vortex axis.  
* The result looks—quite literally—like a *hollow tube* made of the isosurface.  
* In a shear layer the tube can tilt, kink or merge with others; where it impinges on the free surface the top may flare outward, giving the familiar mushroom"” cap shape.

Because the **surface** wraps the entire swirling core, it gives an immediate, geometry-aware picture of where coherent eddies live, how thick they are and how far they persist downstream (see the images above).

### Anatomy of a Q-tube

| Feature on the isosurface | Physical meaning |
|---------------------------|------------------|
| **Tube radius** | Radius where Ω² = S² at the chosen level; a proxy for core size. |
| **Centreline kinks** | Axial stretching / tilting of the vortex by mean shear or neighbouring eddies. |
| **Gradual thinning** | Vortex being strained and entrained—first step of the cascade. |
| **Sudden fragmentation into smaller tubes** | Breakdown into daughter eddies roughly ½–¼ of the parent diameter—evidence you are resolving the inertial range. |
| **Jagged, blocky walls aligned with grid** | Numerical artefact → mesh is too coarse or anisotropic. |

---

### Why we talk about *tubes* instead of *blobs* or *clouds*

* **Galilean-invariant**: a tube traced with Q holds its shape even if you change reference frame, unlike vorticity magnitude.  
* **Topology-preserving**: a single connected component corresponds to *one* vortex core, making it easy to count and track.  
* **Scale-sensitive**: the diameter responds to mesh resolution—hence the idea of using tube breakup as a grid-refinement litmus test.

---

### Practical tips for spotting real versus spurious tubes

| What you see | Interpretation | Suggested action |
|--------------|---------------|------------------|
| Smooth tube that pinches gradually, then splits into 2–4 daughter tubes whose radii are ≳ 6 Δ (local cell size) | **Healthy cascade**: grid is fine enough for at least the first cascade step. | No change, or consider local refinement only if you need the *next* cascade generation. |
| Tube vanishes before the radius approaches ≈ 6 Δ, leaving no offspring | **Over-damping** by numerics (low-order scheme, high filtering) or too-coarse grid. | Switch to a less dissipative scheme, shrink Δ or locally refine. |
| Tube walls show stair-step pattern and break into a pepper"” of tiny cube-shaped blobs | **Grid imprint / aliasing**: anisotropic or under-resolved cells. | Aim for near-isotropic cells (aspect < 3); refine uniformly in all directions. |
| Tubes instantly fragment into blobs the size of one or two cells as soon as they form | **Temporal under-resolution** or aggressive SGS model. | Reduce Δt (CFL ≲ 0.5) or tune SGS coefficients; verify with a smaller time step. |
