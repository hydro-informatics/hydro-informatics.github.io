What the Q-criterion is

What the Q-criterion is¶

For any 3-D velocity field u = (u, v, w) you can split the velocity-gradient tensor

∇u=∂ui∂xj\nabla\mathbf{u}= \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}∇u=∂xj​∂ui​​
(1)

into its symmetric part S (the strain–rate tensor) and antisymmetric part Ω (the rotation/vorticity tensor):

Sij=12 ⁣(∂ui∂xj+∂uj∂xi),Ωij=12 ⁣(∂ui∂xj−∂uj∂xi)S_{ij}=\tfrac12\!\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}+\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right),\qquad \Omega_{ij}=\tfrac12\!\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}-\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)Sij​=21​(∂xj​∂ui​​+∂xi​∂uj​​),Ωij​=21​(∂xj​∂ui​​−∂xi​∂uj​​)
(2)

The second invariant of ∇u,

Q=12 ⁣(∥Ω∥2−∥S∥2)=14(ωiωi−2SijSij)Q=\tfrac12\!\left(\|\Omega\|^{2}-\|S\|^{2}\right) =\tfrac14\bigl(\omega_i\omega_i-2S_{ij}S_{ij}\bigr)Q=21​(∥Ω∥2−∥S∥2)=41​(ωi​ωi​−2Sij​Sij​)
(3)

(where ω = ∇ × u) measures the excess of local rotation over strain.

  • Q > 0 → rotation dominates → a vortex core

  • Q < 0 → strain dominates → shear layers, saddle regions

Because the definition is Galilean-invariant (independent of the observer’s translational motion) it is a robust way to tag coherent vortical structures in unsteady flows.

Why it matters for surface-water turbulence¶

AspectPractical meaning in open-channel / free-surface CFD
Energy cascadeLarge eddies generated at the bed or lateral shear layers show up as Q > 0 tubes or “mushrooms”; watching their breakup helps check whether the grid is fine enough to capture energy down-cascade.
Free-surface imprintVertical vortices emerging from bed roughness often penetrate the free surface; plotting Q iso-surfaces that intersect z = η(x,y,t) reveals air-entrainment pockets or roller footprints in hydraulic jumps.
Mixing & dispersionRegions of persistent positive Q coincide with enhanced vertical exchange; useful for pollutant or sediment modules that rely on turbulent diffusivity estimates.
Model evaluationIn LES/DNS you expect a broad spectrum of small-scale Q structures; if you run RANS, only the time-averaged, larger-scale vortices survive. Comparing the two tells you what the turbulence closure is suppressing.

How to compute Q from your numerical results¶

  • Yes, in an LES or DNS you do want to see the big bed-generated tubes"” (or the mushroom-shaped roller at a lateral shear layer) roll up and progressively fragment into smaller Q-positive packets.

  • If they vanish too early or never break up, the grid (or numerics) is probably damping the inertial cascade; if they fragment instantly into grid-aligned noise"”, the grid is again too coarse (or too anisotropic).

  • Using Q-criterion iso-surfaces at a few fixed thresholds and watching how their size spectrum changes under mesh refinement is a fast, model-agnostic way to judge whether you are resolving enough of the energy cascade.

Tubes: Think of a Q-tube as the skin of a vortex core. Watching how that skin stretches, thins and finally tears tells you whether your numerical mesh is letting the turbulence cascade work naturally—or whether it is being strangled early by numerical diffusion. If the breakup occurs smoothly and at a scale that is still several cells across, you are in good shape; if not, it is a cue to revisit grid density, aspect ratio, or the discretisation scheme (read more below -- at the bottom of this chapter).

Why big tubes should break up¶

Flow regimeWhat really happensWhat you should see in Q-iso surfaces
Fully-turbulent open-channel or estuarine LES/DNSHorseshoe vortices form at the bed, then stretch and undergo the Richardson cascadeA continuous tube that pinches, kinks and spawns a family of smaller tubes downstream
Coarse LES / VLES / WMLESOnly the first one or two cascade steps are resolved; the SGS model removes the restTubes survive but develop a fuzzy outer halo—no pepper"” of tiny blobs
RANS / depth-averaged modelsEverything below the mixing-length is modeledYou will not see breakup; that is expected

So for scale-resolving simulations the breakup is desirable—it means your mesh is letting at least the energy-containing and part of the inertial subrange develop.

2 Quick visual checklist¶

  1. Threshold choice – plot 5 – 25 % of the positive Q-range (or ≈ 1 × Qrms); that keeps focus on energetic cores (No Slide Title).

  2. Coherence length – until the local eddy diameter ≈ 6–10 Δ (cell size) the tube should be smooth; if it dissolves at, say, 20 Δ, numerical diffusion is excessive .

  3. Grid imprint – checkerboard"” Q-surfaces or eddies aligned with I-J-K axes are red flags for anisotropic cells.

  4. Refinement test – double the resolution in the shear-layer zone; if the breakup point moves upstream or the diameter distribution extends to smaller scales, the original mesh was under-resolved.

4 How fine is fine" enough”?—practical numbers¶

  • Integral-scale criterion – aim for at least 10–15 cells across the local integral length‐scale l₀ (≈ 0.4 h in straight rivers; smaller behind sluices) to resolve ≈ 80 % of TKE .

  • Wall resolution – WRLES: y⁺ ≤ 1; WMLES: y⁺ ≤ 40, but keep Δx,z/Δy < 10 to avoid pancake"” cells.

  • Free surface – vertical Δ ≈ h/50 keeps Kelvin–Helmholtz rollers visible right up to the air–water interface.

  • Time step – CFL ≲ 0.5; otherwise tubes break numerically rather than physically.

5 Fixing unwanted behaviour¶

SymptomLikely causeRemedies
Tubes disappear too soon (no small-scale offspring)Excess numerical dissipation (1st-order schemes, over-stretched cells)Switch to central/linear schemes, locally refine, keep aspect ratio < 3
Tubes shatter into grid-aligned glitter"” right after birthΔ too coarse relative to l₀; aliasingIsotropic refinement or adaptive re-meshing driven by *
Breakup point shifts with time-step sizeTemporal under-resolutionCut Δt or use second-order implicit
LESIQ < 0.6 in bed layerSGS model doing all the workRefine until LESIQ ≥ 0.8 or switch to wall-modelled LES

Advanced option: adaptive refinement on Q – many solvers (OpenFOAM dynamicRefineFvMesh, Basilisk adapt_wavelet) let you tag cells where |Q| > α Q′ and keep Δ below a user set maximum; that targets vortices without blowing up cell count elsewhere.

6 Putting it together: a minimal workflow¶

  1. Baseline run with your current grid.

  2. Post-process Q, build the Q_Criterion_Surface_Water.ipynb notebook’s histogram or N(d) spectrum.

  3. Local refinement (×2) in zones where Q>α Qrms.

  4. Repeat run; compare spectra and IQk.

  5. If spectra match and IQk ≳ 0.8, grid is adequate; else iterate.

Following these steps will turn the qualitative mushroom" watching” into a repeatable grid-independence test, and your surface-water LES will stand on solid ground.

Feel free to ping me if you want ready-made Python snippets for any of the metrics above! 2. Filtering / smoothing
A narrow stencil acts like a high-pass filter and may light up numerical wiggles as false vortices. A simple remedy is to Gaussian-smooth Q once before contouring.

  1. Post-processing tools
    ParaView and Tecplot calculate Q internally: Filters → Gradient Of Unstructured Data → Q-Criterion.
    For Python, a few lines with numpy or xarray suffice:

    # u, v, w on 3-D array; dx, dy, dz arrays
du_dx = np.gradient(u, dx, axis=0)
du_dy = np.gradient(u, dy, axis=1)
...
S = 0.5*((du_dx + du_dx.T) + ...)
Omega = 0.5*((du_dx - du_dx.T) + ...)
Q = 0.5*(np.sum(Omega**2,axis=(0,1)) - np.sum(S**2,axis=(0,1)))

  2. Choosing an iso-level
    The conventional choice is Q = 0; in practice you pick Q = α Q′ where Q′ is the domain rms and α ≈ 1–2. That suppresses tiny, non-energetic eddies.

4. Caveats specific to shallow-water / quasi-2-D models¶

  • In depth-averaged (2-DH) simulations, Ω collapses to a scalar vorticity ωz. The full tensor invariants no longer exist, so the Q-criterion is not defined—use ωz or Okubo–Weiss instead.

  • In Stratified rivers or estuaries, vertical shear dominates over spanwise rotation; Q may under-diagnose baroclinic"” vortices tied to density gradients.

  • Near hydraulic structures (piers, gates) flow separation becomes strongly 3-D—here the Q-criterion regains value even if the far-field is essentially shallow-water.

6. Putting it to work¶

  1. Check mesh resolution: a monotone decay of Q iso-surface sizes with grid refinement is a good LES convergence test.

  2. Diagnose bed-generated turbulence: plot Q near z = 0 to see horseshoe and wake vortices from roughness elements.

  3. Couple with scalar fields: overlay dye concentration or TKE; intense mixing occurs where scalar gradients coincide with Q > 0 tubes.

What the tubes"” are

When you plot an isosurface of Q > 0 (pick a fixed threshold such as ≈ 1 × Qrms) you get a three-dimensional boundary that encloses every point where local rotation beats strain.

  • If the underlying vortex is reasonably compact, this boundary has an almost circular cross-section and stretches along the vortex axis.

  • The result looks—quite literally—like a hollow tube made of the isosurface.

  • In a shear layer the tube can tilt, kink or merge with others; where it impinges on the free surface the top may flare outward, giving the familiar mushroom"” cap shape.

Because the surface wraps the entire swirling core, it gives an immediate, geometry-aware picture of where coherent eddies live, how thick they are and how far they persist downstream (see the images above).

Anatomy of a Q-tube¶

Feature on the isosurfacePhysical meaning
Tube radiusRadius where Ω² = S² at the chosen level; a proxy for core size.
Centreline kinksAxial stretching / tilting of the vortex by mean shear or neighbouring eddies.
Gradual thinningVortex being strained and entrained—first step of the cascade.
Sudden fragmentation into smaller tubesBreakdown into daughter eddies roughly ½–¼ of the parent diameter—evidence you are resolving the inertial range.
Jagged, blocky walls aligned with gridNumerical artefact → mesh is too coarse or anisotropic.

Why we talk about tubes instead of blobs or clouds¶

  • Galilean-invariant: a tube traced with Q holds its shape even if you change reference frame, unlike vorticity magnitude.

  • Topology-preserving: a single connected component corresponds to one vortex core, making it easy to count and track.

  • Scale-sensitive: the diameter responds to mesh resolution—hence the idea of using tube breakup as a grid-refinement litmus test.

Practical tips for spotting real versus spurious tubes¶

What you seeInterpretationSuggested action
Smooth tube that pinches gradually, then splits into 2–4 daughter tubes whose radii are ≳ 6 Δ (local cell size)Healthy cascade: grid is fine enough for at least the first cascade step.No change, or consider local refinement only if you need the next cascade generation.
Tube vanishes before the radius approaches ≈ 6 Δ, leaving no offspringOver-damping by numerics (low-order scheme, high filtering) or too-coarse grid.Switch to a less dissipative scheme, shrink Δ or locally refine.
Tube walls show stair-step pattern and break into a pepper"” of tiny cube-shaped blobsGrid imprint / aliasing: anisotropic or under-resolved cells.Aim for near-isotropic cells (aspect < 3); refine uniformly in all directions.
Tubes instantly fragment into blobs the size of one or two cells as soon as they formTemporal under-resolution or aggressive SGS model.Reduce Δt (CFL ≲ 0.5) or tune SGS coefficients; verify with a smaller time step.
